In recent years, tipping in the United States has become an increasingly common topic on social media.

Someone posts a photo of a bubble tea shop’s POS terminal, prominently displaying options for 20%, 25%, and 30%. Someone else complains that a restaurant has already added a Service Charge, only for a Tip prompt to appear at checkout. Others have noticed that even coffee shops, delivery platforms, and self-service counters now ask whether they would like to leave a tip.

The comments tend to express the same sentiment:

> “Tipping is getting ridiculous.”

At first, I simply regarded it as part of American consumer culture.

But eventually, I realized that what truly confused me was not the shift from 15% to 20%, or the steadily rising percentages suggested on the screen. It was a different question:

**Am I saying thank you, or am I paying someone else’s wages?**

That question made me reconsider the American tipping system as a whole.

---

## Not All the Money on the Bill Is the Same

Before discussing tipping, we first need to distinguish among several charges that often appear on the same bill.

The menu price covers the product and the basic service that comes with it.

A Service Charge is a mandatory fee set in advance by the business. Under US federal law, a mandatory Service Charge is not considered a Tip. Whether and how that money is distributed to employees depends on the business’s policies and local regulations.[^1]

A Tip, in principle, is voluntary. The customer decides whether to leave one, how much to leave, and who receives it.

Then there is something that occupies the space between law and psychology: the Suggested Tip.

Legally, it may still be voluntary. But when a POS terminal places 20%, 25%, and 30% in the most conspicuous spots, hides “No Tip” in a corner, or makes the customer choose in front of an employee, that voluntary choice comes with unmistakable psychological pressure.

So when a Service Charge, a Suggested Tip, and a genuinely voluntary Tip all appear on the same bill, the customer’s discomfort may not come merely from paying more.

The deeper problem is this:

**I am not even sure what I am paying for.**

---

## How Tipping Went from Gratitude to Part of a Wage

Modern tipping is generally traced back to Europe’s master-servant society.

Masters, guests, or travelers would give gratuities to servants who provided extra service. In the nineteenth century, wealthy Americans traveling through Europe brought the custom home.

In principle, tipping at the time had at least two defining features:

First, it was voluntary.

Second, it was a reward on top of ordinary compensation.

Interestingly, American society did not initially embrace the practice.

From the late nineteenth to the early twentieth century, many Americans viewed tipping as deeply aristocratic. It implied a hierarchy between the person bestowing charity and the person receiving it, which conflicted with American ideals of equality. Some states even passed laws restricting or prohibiting tipping.[^2]

After the Civil War, however, the American labor market and service industries were also changing.

Large numbers of newly freed Black Americans entered industries such as restaurants, hotels, and railroads. Some employers paid these workers no regular wages—or very little—on the assumption that customers’ tips would provide their income.

Racial history, the growth of the service sector, commercial interests, and consumer habits all contributed to the spread of tipping in the United States.

A tip was no longer merely a reward on top of ordinary wages. It gradually became part of workers’ compensation.

In 1966, federal law formally introduced what is known as the tip credit. Eligible employers could count a portion of employees’ tips toward their minimum-wage obligations.[^3]

Under current federal standards, qualifying employers may pay tipped employees a direct cash wage of as little as $2.13 per hour and use tips to make up the difference between that amount and the federal minimum wage. If an employee’s cash wages and tips combined do not reach the minimum wage, the employer is legally required to cover the shortfall.

State rules vary, however. Some states require employers to pay tipped employees the full state minimum wage before tips.[^4]

Legally, a tip remains money that a customer voluntarily chooses to pay.

Economically, however, it has become embedded in the wage system.

Tipping has therefore acquired two identities:

It is both an expression of gratitude and part of a wage.

---

## Why Does Tipping Feel Increasingly Uncomfortable Today?

If tipping had remained nothing more than a thank-you on top of regular compensation, most people probably would not resent it.

The real problem is that tipping now plays two different roles at once.

On the one hand, it is still described this way:

> “It is the customer’s voluntary expression of gratitude.”

On the other hand, it makes up a significant portion of many service workers’ income.

That creates a strange dilemma.

If I do not tip, have I merely declined to express extra appreciation, or have I deprived someone of part of their expected income?

If the service was merely ordinary, am I evaluating the service or punishing the employee?

If the business has already added a Service Charge, am I still supposed to leave a Tip?

Several fundamentally different questions are compressed into a single choice on a checkout screen.

Choose not to tip, and you feel stingy.

Choose to tip, and you may still wonder whether you are offering a voluntary thank-you or paying labor costs that were left out of the menu price.

A wage relationship that should exist between employer and employee has gradually been turned into psychological pressure between employee and customer.

What has changed about tipping, then, is not merely the amount.

It is the role tipping has been made to play.

---

## The Real Question Is Who Sets Wages—and Who Guarantees Them

When people discuss tipping, they often ask:

Why can restaurant servers receive tips while bank tellers cannot?

Why can Uber drivers receive them while convenience-store cashiers cannot?

Why are tips expected in some occupations but not accepted at all in others that require a similar amount of time and service?

These are certainly questions of fairness.

But before asking whether these differences among occupations are fair, we should ask a more fundamental question:

**Who should determine workers’ compensation, and who should guarantee it?**

Under any system, consumers ultimately pay the cost of labor.

If a restaurant raises its employees’ wages, those costs may still be reflected in menu prices, service fees, or other charges. Making employers responsible for wages does not mean consumers will no longer bear labor costs.

The real difference is how those costs are incorporated into the price consumers pay.

One approach is to include the full cost of compensating employees for their ordinary duties transparently in the stated price, with the employer setting wages and guaranteeing employees’ income.

The other assumes, in both pricing and compensation, that customers will leave an additional tip. Part of the employee’s expected income is left unresolved until checkout, when the customer decides whether to make up the difference.

Under the first system, customers pay for goods and services through the menu price. They do not need to supplement employees’ wages with tips.

Under the second, customers first pay the stated bill and then face another choice—nominally voluntary, but consequential for the employee’s income.

The issue, then, is not whether consumers should bear labor costs.

The issue is this:

**Consumers should pay one clear price. They should not have to pay for goods and services first, then be asked, through moral pressure, to cover wages that were never included in the stated price.**

If the menu price already covers what employees should earn for performing their ordinary duties, customers should not, in principle, also be expected to tip.

Any additional money should be an expression of gratitude, not a way to make up wages.

That is the real difference between the two systems.

Consumers ultimately pay under both, but only the first makes the price and the responsibility clear:

The business includes its true costs in the price.

The employer sets and guarantees employees’ income.

The customer pays the amount disclosed in advance.

---

## If the Problems Are So Obvious, Why Does Tipping Still Exist?

If the tipping system is so riddled with contradictions, why doesn’t the American restaurant industry simply include employees’ full wages in its prices?

One reason is that lower menu prices are more attractive.

If a restaurant raises wages and builds the cost into its food prices, it may appear more expensive than a nearby restaurant that still relies on tips.

Even if customers ultimately pay roughly the same total, the first numbers they see when choosing a restaurant are usually the prices on the menu.

Another reason is that not every server wants tipping to disappear.

At busy restaurants with high average checks, some servers may earn more from tips than they would under a fixed-wage model.

For them, tipping brings not only instability but also the possibility of higher earnings.

Customers may also appreciate the sense of control that tipping provides.

If the service is good, they can leave more; if it is poor, they can leave less. Tipping makes customers feel that they have the power to evaluate and reward service.

At the same time, the entire industry has developed a stable set of expectations.

Customers know that restaurant meals usually call for tips. Employees choose jobs based on expected tip income. Businesses set wages and menu prices on the assumption that customers will tip.

Even if everyone involved is dissatisfied with the system, no individual restaurant, employee, or customer can easily opt out alone.

A restaurant that unilaterally raises prices and eliminates tipping may look more expensive than its competitors.

An employee who rejects the tipping system may lose the opportunity to earn more.

A customer who decides not to tip will usually hurt the server standing in front of them, not the system itself.

Tipping is therefore more than a payment habit.

It has become a system of mutual dependence.

Once such a system takes shape, whether it is reasonable and whether it is easy to change become two separate questions.

But the fact that a system is difficult to leave does not make all its rules reasonable.

Even if we accept that tipping will continue for now, we still need to ask: How should a tip be calculated?

---

## Is a Percentage Really the Most Reasonable Method?

Why are tips usually calculated as a percentage of the amount spent?

Imagine two customers.

One orders a $20 hamburger.

The other orders a $200 steak.

At 20%, one leaves a $4 tip and the other $40.

Was the service really worth ten times as much?

The same problem arises with rides.

A trip along the same route may cost $20 one time and $80 another because of traffic or surge pricing. The driver may not have provided four times the service, but the suggested tip rises with the fare when calculated as a percentage.

Of course, the total amount spent is not entirely meaningless.

A more expensive restaurant may involve a longer meal, more detailed explanations of the dishes, higher service expectations, and more employees taking part in the service.

Percentage-based tipping is also simple. Customers do not have to estimate from scratch how much labor a service worker provided each time.

The amount spent can therefore sometimes serve as a rough indicator of service complexity.

But it remains only a proxy.

It may reflect the time spent providing service, the type of restaurant, and the number of diners. Or it may simply reflect the cost of ingredients, alcohol, surge pricing, or a brand premium.

**The price of a product may be related to the value of the service, but the two are not equivalent.**

A percentage is a convenient, standardized rule.

But convenience does not make it an accurate measure of the labor itself.

If the role of tipping is unclear and the way it is calculated is only a rough proxy, then what needs to be redesigned is not merely the percentage. It is the relationship among wages, prices, and gratitude.

---

## If We Redesigned the Tipping System

I do not believe tipping needs to disappear entirely.

What truly needs reconsideration is the role it should play.

In my view, redesigning the system could begin with three principles.

First, the labor cost of basic service should be included in the stated price.

If taking orders, bringing food, pouring water, and processing payments are ordinary parts of the job, then the menu price—or a clearly disclosed service fee—should cover the compensation employees deserve for performing those duties.

That may mean higher menu prices.

But a higher stated price does not necessarily mean consumers will pay more overall. It may simply make explicit a cost that was previously hidden in the tip.

Reforming the tipping system does not mean asking owners to absorb a cost that will somehow never be passed on to consumers.

It means requiring businesses to disclose their true costs and take responsibility for their employees’ income.

Second, employers should guarantee employees a basic income.

Neither a slow day nor one customer’s willingness to tip should determine whether an employee receives basic compensation.

Customers may evaluate service, but they should not be responsible for calculating wages or guaranteeing income.

Third, tips may remain, but they should return to being voluntary expressions of gratitude.

If service clearly exceeds ordinary expectations—for example, if someone proactively assists an elderly customer, patiently handles a complicated request, or creates a genuinely satisfying experience—the customer can certainly offer an additional thank-you.

In that case, the tip is a reward beyond normal compensation, not a hole in the employee’s income waiting for a customer to fill it.

Nor does the amount necessarily need to be calculated as a percentage of the purchase.

A fixed amount, rounding up, or choosing an amount based on the extra effort involved may all come closer to expressing genuine gratitude.

What truly deserves to be eliminated may not be tipping itself.

It is tipping’s function as a hidden wage, along with a payment process that is voluntary in name but fraught with pressure in practice.

---

## Final Thoughts

When people discuss tipping, the conversation often revolves around one word:

**Fairness.**

Why can that person receive tips while I cannot?

Why should a $200 meal require ten times the tip of a $20 meal?

Why do similar services follow completely different rules in different settings?

All of these questions are worth discussing.

But before we consider fairness, there may be a more fundamental question:

**Who should determine compensation for labor, and who should guarantee it?**

A good system does not necessarily require everyone to pay the same amount.

But it should make prices as transparent and responsibilities as clear as possible. It should also assure workers that their basic income does not depend on whether someone else feels guilty at checkout.

The business includes its true costs in the price.

The employer sets and guarantees employees’ income.

The customer pays the amount disclosed in advance.

Then, if the customer wishes, they may offer an additional expression of gratitude.

Consumers should pay one clear price for goods and services. They should not pay the bill and then be pressured to cover wages that were never included in the stated price.

When gratitude becomes an obligation, it is no longer gratitude.

When wages increasingly depend on a customer’s split-second decision, the money is no longer merely a tip.

So perhaps the question truly worth asking is not:

**“How much should I tip?”**

It is:

**“At this moment, am I saying thank you—or paying someone else’s wages?”**
