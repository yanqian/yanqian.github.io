In recent years, tipping in the United States has become an increasingly common topic on social media.

People post photos of bubble tea shop POS screens prominently displaying tip options of 20%, 25%, and 30%. Some complain that a restaurant has already added a Service Charge, only for a Tip line to appear when it is time to pay. Others have noticed that even coffee shops, delivery platforms, and self-service counters now ask whether they would like to leave a tip.

The prevailing sentiment in the comments is remarkably consistent:

> “Tipping is getting more and more ridiculous.”

At first, I simply regarded it as part of American consumer culture.

But eventually, I realized that what truly confused me was not that tips had risen from 15% to 20%, or that the suggested amounts on the screen kept increasing. It was a different question:

**Am I saying thank you, or am I paying someone else’s wages?**

That question led me to reconsider the American tipping system.

---

## Not All the Money on Your Bill Is the Same

Before discussing tips, we first need to distinguish among several charges that often appear together on a bill.

The menu price covers the product and the basic service that comes with it.

A Service Charge is a mandatory fee set in advance by the business. Under U.S. federal law, a mandatory Service Charge is not considered a Tip. Whether and how that money is distributed to employees depends on the business’s policies and local regulations.[^1]

A Tip, by contrast, is in principle left to the customer’s discretion. Customers may decide whether to tip, how much to leave, and who should receive it.

Then there is something that falls between legal voluntariness and psychological pressure: the Suggested Tip.

Legally, it may still be voluntary. But when a POS screen puts 20%, 25%, and 30% in the most prominent positions, hides “No Tip” in a corner, or requires customers to make their choice in front of an employee, that choice comes with unmistakable pressure.

So when a Service Charge, a Suggested Tip, and a genuinely voluntary Tip all appear on the same bill, the customer’s discomfort may not simply be about paying more.

The deeper problem is this:

**I am not even sure what I am paying for.**

---

## How Tipping Went From Gratitude to Part of a Wage

Modern tipping is generally traced to Europe’s master-servant society.

Masters, guests, or travelers would give gratuities to servants who provided extra service. In the nineteenth century, wealthy Americans returning from Europe brought the custom home with them.

At least in principle, tipping at the time had two defining features:

First, it was voluntary.

Second, it was a reward on top of normal compensation.

Interestingly, American society did not initially embrace the practice.

From the late nineteenth to the early twentieth century, many Americans saw tipping as deeply aristocratic. It implied a hierarchy between the person bestowing a favor and the person receiving it, clashing with American ideals of equality. Some states even passed laws restricting or banning tips.[^2]

But after the Civil War, the American labor market and service sector were also changing.

Large numbers of newly freed Black Americans entered industries such as restaurants, hotels, and railroads. Some employers paid these workers no regular wage, or only a very low one, on the assumption that they would rely on customers’ tips for their income.

Racial history, the growth of the service sector, commercial interests, and consumer habits all contributed to the spread of tipping.

A tip was no longer merely a reward above and beyond wages. It gradually became part of workers’ compensation.

In 1966, federal law formally introduced what is known as the tip credit. Eligible employers could count a portion of the tips their employees received toward their minimum-wage obligations.[^3]

Under current federal standards, qualifying employers may pay tipped employees a direct cash wage as low as $2.13 per hour, then count tips toward the federal minimum wage. If cash wages plus tips do not reach the minimum wage, the employer is legally required to make up the difference.

State rules vary, however. Some states require employers to pay the full state minimum wage in addition to any tips employees receive.[^4]

Legally, a tip remains money that a customer voluntarily chooses to pay.

Economically, however, it has been built into the wage system.

Tipping has therefore acquired two identities:

It is both an expression of gratitude and a portion of someone’s wages.

---

## Why Does Tipping Feel Increasingly Uncomfortable Today?

If a tip had remained nothing more than a thank-you on top of regular compensation, most people probably would not resent it.

The real problem is that tipping now plays two different roles at once.

On the one hand, it is still described this way:

> “It is the customer’s voluntary expression of gratitude.”

On the other hand, it accounts for a significant share of many service workers’ income.

That creates a strange dilemma.

If I do not tip, am I simply declining to offer an extra thank-you, or am I depriving someone of part of their expected income?

If the service is merely average, am I evaluating the service or punishing the employee?

If the business has already added a Service Charge, do I still need to leave a Tip?

Several fundamentally different questions have been compressed into a single choice on a checkout screen.

Decline, and you feel stingy.

Accept, and you may still be unsure whether the money is a voluntary thank-you or a labor cost omitted from the menu price.

A wage relationship that should exist between employer and employee has gradually become a source of psychological pressure between employee and customer.

So what has truly changed about tipping is not merely the amount.

It is the role tipping has been made to play.

---

## The Real Question Is Who Sets Wages and Who Guarantees Income

Discussions about tipping often raise questions like these:

Why can a restaurant server receive tips while a bank teller cannot?

Why can an Uber driver receive tips but not a convenience-store cashier?

Why are tips expected in some occupations but not accepted at all in others, even when the services take roughly the same amount of time?

These are certainly questions of fairness.

But before asking whether it is fair for some occupations to receive tips and not others, we should ask a more fundamental question:

**Who should determine compensation for work, and who should guarantee it?**

Under any system, consumers ultimately bear the cost of labor.

If a restaurant raises its employees’ wages, that cost may still be reflected in menu prices, service fees, or other charges. Making employers responsible for wages does not mean consumers will somehow stop bearing labor costs.

The real difference is how those costs are built into the price consumers pay.

One approach is to include the full compensation employees deserve for their regular work transparently in the stated price, with employers setting wages and guaranteeing employees’ income.

The other is to set prices and compensation on the assumption that customers will leave additional tips, deferring part of employees’ expected income until checkout and leaving customers to decide whether to fill the gap.

Under the first system, customers pay for goods and services through the menu price. They are not expected to use tips to complete an employee’s wages.

Under the second, customers first pay the amount on the bill, then face another choice—nominally voluntary, but consequential to an employee’s income.

The issue, then, is not whether consumers should bear labor costs.

The issue is this:

**Consumers should pay one clearly stated price. They should not first pay for the product and service, then be asked, through moral pressure, to cover wages that were never included in the price.**

If the menu price already covers the compensation employees deserve for doing their regular jobs, customers should not, in principle, be expected to tip.

Any extra money left at that point should express gratitude, not make up for a wage shortfall.

That is the real difference between the two systems.

Consumers ultimately fund both, but only the first makes both the price and the division of responsibility clear:

The business is responsible for including its true costs in the price.

The employer is responsible for setting and guaranteeing employees’ income.

The customer is responsible for paying the amount disclosed in advance.

## Is a Percentage Really the Most Reasonable Method?

Even if we accept tipping as a system, another question remains:

Why are tips usually calculated as a percentage of the purchase price?

Imagine two diners.

One orders a $20 hamburger.

The other orders a $200 steak.

At 20%, one leaves a $4 tip and the other leaves $40.

Was the second diner’s service really worth ten times as much?

The same issue arises with rides.

The same trip might cost $20 one day and $80 another because of traffic or surge pricing. The driver may not have provided four times the service, but the suggested percentage-based tip rises along with the fare.

Of course, the amount spent is not entirely meaningless.

A more expensive restaurant may involve a longer meal, more detailed explanations of the dishes, higher service expectations, and more employees taking part in the service.

A percentage is also simple. Customers do not have to estimate from scratch how much work the service employee did every time they pay.

Spending can therefore sometimes serve as a rough indicator of service complexity.

But it remains only a proxy.

It may reflect the length of the service, the type of restaurant, or the size of the party. Or it may reflect nothing more than ingredient costs, alcohol prices, surge pricing, or a brand premium.

**The price of a product may be related to the value of the service, but the two are not the same.**

A percentage is a convenient, standardized rule.

But convenience does not mean it accurately measures the labor involved.

---

## If the Problems Are So Obvious, Why Does Tipping Persist?

If the tipping system contains so many contradictions, why doesn’t the American restaurant industry simply build employees’ full wages into its prices?

One reason is that lower menu prices are more appealing.

A restaurant that raises wages and incorporates the added cost into its menu prices may appear more expensive than a nearby restaurant that still relies on tips.

Even if customers ultimately pay roughly the same total, the first thing they usually see when choosing a restaurant is the price on the menu.

Another reason is that not every server wants tipping to disappear.

At busy restaurants with high average checks, some servers may earn more in tips than they would under a fixed-wage model.

For them, tipping means not only instability but also the potential for higher earnings.

Customers may also enjoy the sense of control that tipping provides.

If the service is good, they can leave more. If it is poor, they can leave less. Tipping makes customers feel that they have the power to evaluate and reward service.

At the same time, the entire industry has settled into a stable set of expectations.

Customers know that restaurants generally expect tips. Employees choose jobs based on expected tip income. Businesses set wages and menu prices on the assumption that customers will tip.

Even if everyone involved is dissatisfied with the system, it is difficult for any individual restaurant, employee, or customer to opt out alone.

A restaurant that unilaterally raises prices and eliminates tipping may look more expensive than its competitors.

An employee who rejects the tipping system may lose the opportunity to earn more.

A customer who decides not to tip will usually hurt the server standing in front of them, not the system itself.

Tipping is therefore more than a payment habit.

It has become a system of mutual dependence.

And once a system has taken hold, whether it is reasonable and whether it is easy to change are often two different questions.

---

## If We Redesigned the Tipping System

I do not believe tipping necessarily has to disappear altogether.

What needs to be reconsidered is the role it should play.

I would begin with three principles.

First, the labor cost of basic service should be included in the stated price.

If taking orders, serving food, refilling water, and processing payments are normal parts of a job, then the menu price or a clearly disclosed service fee should cover the compensation employees deserve for that work.

This may mean higher menu prices.

But a higher listed price does not necessarily mean consumers will pay more overall. It may simply mean disclosing up front costs that were previously hidden in tips.

Reforming tipping does not mean demanding that business owners absorb costs that will never be passed on to consumers.

It means requiring businesses to reflect their true costs in their prices and take responsibility for their employees’ income.

Second, employees’ basic income should be guaranteed by their employers.

Neither slow business nor an individual customer’s willingness to tip should determine whether an employee receives basic compensation.

Customers may evaluate service, but they should not be responsible for calculating wages or guaranteeing income.

Third, tipping can remain, but it should return to being a voluntary expression of gratitude.

If service clearly exceeds normal expectations—for example, if an employee proactively assists an older person, patiently resolves a complicated request, or provides a genuinely satisfying experience—the customer should, of course, be free to offer an additional thank-you.

In that situation, the tip is a reward beyond normal compensation, not a gap in the employee’s income that the customer is expected to fill.

Nor must the amount necessarily be calculated as a percentage of the purchase.

A fixed amount, rounding up, or an amount based on the extra effort involved may all come closer to expressing “thank you.”

What truly deserves to be eliminated may not be tipping itself.

It may be tipping’s role as a hidden wage, along with a payment process that is voluntary in name but saturated with pressure in practice.

---

## Final Thoughts

When people discuss tipping, the conversation often revolves around one word:

**Fairness.**

Why can one person receive tips while another cannot?

Why should a $200 meal require a tip ten times larger than a $20 meal?

Why do similar services follow completely different rules in different settings?

All of these questions deserve consideration.

But before we address fairness, there may be an even more fundamental question:

**Who should determine compensation for work, and who should guarantee it?**

A good system does not necessarily require everyone to pay the same amount.

But it should make prices as transparent and responsibilities as clear as possible. It should also allow workers to know that their basic income does not depend on whether someone else feels guilty at checkout.

The business is responsible for building its true costs into the price.

The employer is responsible for setting and guaranteeing employees’ income.

The customer is responsible for paying the amount disclosed in advance.

And, if they wish, offering an additional thank-you.

Consumers should pay one clearly stated price for goods and services, rather than paying the bill and then being pressured to cover wages that were never included in the price.

When gratitude becomes an obligation, it is no longer gratitude.

When wages increasingly depend on a customer’s decision in the moment, they are no longer merely tips.

So perhaps the question truly worth asking is not:

**“How much should I tip?”**

But:

**“At this moment, am I saying thank you, or am I paying wages on someone else’s behalf?”**
